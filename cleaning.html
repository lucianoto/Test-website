Section 3

Cleaning, Sanitation & Maintenance

A device that is not cleaned properly stops being treatment and starts being a delivery system for infection. This section covers what to do after every session, how to disinfect, how to dry and store, and when to replace parts.

Confirm against your manufacturer's instructions

The protocol below reflects general cystic fibrosis infection-control practice. Your specific device will have its own instructions covering which parts may be immersed, which tolerate heat, and which must never get wet. Where the two differ, the manufacturer wins. Sections marked device-specific still need to be completed for this device.

Why this matters more in CF than almost anywhere else

Any device that holds moisture and touches the airway is a potential reservoir. In CF this is not a theoretical concern: the organisms that colonise CF lungs are precisely the ones that thrive in damp equipment.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa lives happily in standing water and residual droplets. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria are present in many domestic water supplies and resist ordinary disinfection. Burkholderia cepacia complex is transmissible between people and hard to treat once established. A device left damp overnight can put an inhaled dose of any of them straight into the lung the next morning — bypassing every upper-airway defence.

Once these organisms become chronic they are usually impossible to eradicate. Cleaning is the part of CF care where a few minutes of routine effort has consequences measured in years.

Four rules that are never negotiable
  • Never share a device, mouthpiece, mask, or tubing with anyone — including a sibling or partner who also has CF.
  • Never store parts damp. Residual moisture is the single most common cause of contamination.
  • Never rinse disinfected parts with tap water. That reintroduces exactly the waterborne organisms you just removed. Use sterile or distilled water.
  • Never use vinegar as a disinfectant. Acetic acid does not reliably kill the organisms that matter in CF, and is no longer recommended.

Daily cleaning — after every single use

Cleaning removes visible residue, medication deposits, and the biological material that disinfection would otherwise have to fight through. It is a separate step from disinfection, and it comes first — disinfecting a dirty part does not work.

  1. Wash your hands

    Soap and warm water, at least 20 seconds, before touching any part. Dry on a clean towel or paper towel. If you use alcohol hand gel instead, let it dry fully.

  2. Take the device fully apart

    Separate every component the manufacturer says is removable. Parts left assembled trap moisture and residue at the joints, which is where growth starts.

    Device-specific: the exact list of removable parts for this device goes here.

  3. Rinse under running water

    Rinse away medication residue immediately, while it is still wet. Dried residue is considerably harder to shift and can clog small openings.

  4. Wash in warm soapy water

    Use a mild liquid dish soap and warm water. Wash each part individually, paying attention to interior surfaces, small channels, and anywhere two surfaces meet. Use a soft brush kept solely for this purpose — never a kitchen sponge, which is itself heavily colonised.

  5. Rinse thoroughly

    Soap residue left behind irritates the airway and can interfere with disinfection. Rinse until no film or bubbles remain.

  6. Disinfect, or move to drying

    If today is a disinfection day, go straight to the disinfection protocol while the parts are still wet. Otherwise, go to drying and storage.

Parts that must never be immersed

Motors, compressors, control units, power supplies, and most tubing must not be submerged. Wipe their exterior with a cloth lightly dampened with water or an approved wipe, and let them dry fully before use.

Disinfection protocol

Disinfection kills the organisms that washing leaves behind. Choose one method below — they are alternatives, not a sequence. Check first that every part you are about to disinfect tolerates that method; heat will deform some plastics and some chemicals degrade seals and membranes.

How often: guidance varies by device and by care centre — commonly after every use, or at least daily. Follow whichever of your manufacturer's instructions or your care team's advice is more frequent.

Heat methods

Heat-based disinfection — preferred where parts tolerate it, because there is no chemical residue to rinse away
Method How Watch out for
Boiling Submerge parts fully in a pot of water, bring to a rolling boil, and boil for 5 minutes from the moment boiling starts. Use a pot kept for this purpose. Parts must be fully underwater and must not rest against the hot base of the pot. Not suitable for heat-sensitive components. Timing starts at the boil, not when the pot goes on.
Microwave steam bag Add water to the bag as printed on it, insert parts, and microwave for the time and wattage the bag specifies (commonly ~5 minutes). Follow the bag, not this page — times differ by brand and microwave power. Bags have a limited number of cycles; track your usage. Contents are scalding on removal.
Electric steam steriliser A countertop baby-bottle steam steriliser, run on its full cycle. Descale the unit regularly as the manufacturer directs — scale harbours bacteria. Do not open early.
Dishwasher Only if the water reaches at least 70 °C (158 °F) and parts stay there for 30 minutes — typically a hot or sanitise cycle with heated drying. Many domestic dishwashers do not reach this reliably. Place small parts in a closed basket so they cannot fall. If you cannot verify the temperature, use another method.

Cold chemical methods

Use these when parts cannot tolerate heat. Every chemical method must be followed by a rinse with sterile or distilled water — never tap water — and then complete air-drying.

Cold chemical disinfection — always followed by a sterile-water rinse
Agent Concentration Soak time Notes
Isopropyl alcohol 70% 5 minutes Evaporates quickly, leaves little residue. Flammable — keep away from heat and use in a ventilated space. Can cloud or crack some plastics over time.
Ethyl alcohol 70–84% 5 minutes Equivalent to isopropyl in practice. Same flammability caution.
Hydrogen peroxide 3% (standard pharmacy strength) 30 minutes Widely available and inexpensive. Degrades with light and age — replace the bottle regularly and never reuse a soak solution.
Proprietary cold-soak solutions As labelled As labelled Only if your device manufacturer approves the specific product. Dilution and contact time are product-specific and must be followed exactly.
Do not use these
  • Vinegar or acetic acid — ineffective against several key CF pathogens. Older guidance recommended it; current guidance does not.
  • Household bleach, unless your manufacturer explicitly specifies it — it corrodes many device materials and residue is harmful to inhale.
  • Reused soak solution — mix fresh every time. A used soak is a contaminated soak.
  • Scented or antibacterial dish soaps — residues are difficult to rinse fully and can irritate the airway.
Clinical detail: why the sterile-water rinse is not optional

Domestic water supplies routinely contain Pseudomonas species and non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), particularly Mycobacterium abscessus and M. avium complex. NTM are notably resistant to chlorine at potable concentrations and readily establish in plumbing biofilms, showerheads, and taps.

Rinsing a chemically disinfected part under the tap therefore reinoculates it with precisely the organisms hardest to treat should they reach a CF airway — NTM disease in CF often requires multi-drug regimens over 12 months or more, with substantial toxicity and uncertain success. Sterile water, or water that has been boiled and cooled, avoids this. Distilled water is an acceptable substitute where sterile water is impractical or unaffordable.

The same reasoning applies to the water used to fill a nebuliser or humidifier reservoir, and to the water in a steam steriliser — the last of these is generally mitigated by the sterilising cycle itself, but descaling still matters.

Drying and storage

This is the step most often rushed, and the one where most contamination actually happens. A perfectly disinfected part put away damp is worse than one that was never disinfected at all — it is now a warm, wet, nutrient-free but perfectly habitable container.

  1. Shake off excess water

    Shake each part to clear droplets from interior channels and corners. Some devices allow you to reconnect the air source briefly and run air through to blow the parts dry — check whether yours does.

  2. Lay parts out on a clean, absorbent surface

    A fresh paper towel, or a lint-free cloth used only for this. Space parts apart so air circulates around each one, and leave them disassembled.

  3. Air-dry completely

    Let them dry fully — usually an hour or more. Do not rub dry with a cloth or tea towel: fabric sheds fibres and carries bacteria, and it will recontaminate everything you just disinfected.

  4. Choose the drying location carefully

    Clean, dry, dust-free, and out of direct traffic. Not the bathroom — humidity, aerosol from the toilet, and mould spores all make it the worst room in the house for this. Not next to a sink, a bin, a pet bed, or an open window over a street.

  5. Store dry, covered, and assembled only if the manufacturer says so

    Once bone dry, store parts in a clean sealed container or a zip bag kept for the purpose. Keep them out of the bathroom and away from heat, sunlight, and damp. Replace the storage container or bag regularly, and wash reusable containers.

Stop using a part immediately if you see
  • Any black, green, pink, or fuzzy growth — that is mould or bacterial colonisation
  • A film, slime, or persistent smell that cleaning does not remove
  • Cloudiness, crazing, cracks, stickiness, or warping in the plastic
  • Perished, hardened, or split seals and gaskets

Replace the part. Do not try to salvage it with a longer soak — a damaged surface holds biofilm in microscopic cracks that no home disinfection can reach.

Component replacement schedule

Cleaning does not make parts last forever. Repeated heat, chemicals, and mechanical stress degrade plastics, membranes, and seals — and a degraded part performs worse long before it visibly fails. Filters and consumables are the cheapest part of CF care and the least worth economising on.

Component Typical interval Replace sooner if
Mouthpiece Every 3–6 months Cracked, discoloured, deformed, or bite marks present
Mask Every 3–6 months Seal hardened or split, strap perished, no longer seals to the face
Medication cup / chamber Per manufacturer — often 3–6 months, or a stated number of uses Cloudy, cracked, or output visibly reduced

Keeping track

  • Write the date you first used a part on a small label or on its storage bag.
  • Set a recurring calendar reminder for each replacement interval.
  • Order replacements before you run out — supply delays are common, and a worn part in use is a compromise you did not choose.
  • Ask your care team what your insurer, health service, or programme covers, and how often. Many cover replacements more generously than people assume.

At a glance

The routine in one table
When Do this
Before every use Wash hands. Check parts are clean, dry, and undamaged.
After every use Disassemble, rinse, wash in warm soapy water, rinse again, air-dry completely.
Daily (or as directed) Disinfect by one approved method. Rinse with sterile or distilled water if a chemical method was used. Air-dry completely.
Weekly Wipe down the main unit and its exterior. Inspect tubing and seals. Check the filter. Clean the storage container.
On schedule Replace filters, seals, tubing, mouthpieces, and masks. Reorder consumables.
At every clinic visit Bring the device or ask your team to review your technique and cleaning routine.
