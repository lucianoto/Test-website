How CF-ARIA Works

Development status

CF-ARIA is a research prototype under active development. It has not been approved or cleared by any regulatory authority, is not available to patients, and must not be used to diagnose or treat cystic fibrosis.

Evidence

The reference standard

Any claim CF-ARIA makes has to be measured against quantitative pilocarpine iontophoresis, the established diagnostic standard for cystic fibrosis. A well-performed sweat test remains the benchmark, and the thresholds are unambiguous: a sweat chloride concentration of 60 mmol/L or above supports a diagnosis of CF, 30 to 59 mmol/L is intermediate and requires further evaluation, and 29 mmol/L or below makes CF unlikely at any age.1

Those numbers matter for two reasons. They are what a transdermal device must agree with, and the intermediate band is where agreement is hardest and most consequential.

What validation has to establish

The following is the standard CF-ARIA is being held to. Presenting it before results exist is deliberate: it states in advance what would count as success, so that later data can be judged against a fixed target rather than a moving one.

Validation requirements and current status
What must be shown Why it matters Status
Analytical accuracy Agreement between the device's chloride measurement and paired laboratory values, reported as Bland-Altman limits of agreement rather than correlation alone. Correlation can look excellent while agreement is clinically unusable. In progress
Diagnostic performance Sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values against quantitative pilocarpine iontophoresis, reported separately for the intermediate 30 to 59 mmol/L band where misclassification is most likely. In progress
Adequate sample rate The proportion of attempts yielding sufficient sweat, particularly in infants, where insufficient volume is the dominant failure mode of conventional sweat testing. In progress
Reproducibility Agreement between repeated measurements on the same subject, and between operators with differing levels of training. In progress
Performance in the target setting Results obtained under the ambient temperature, humidity, and staffing conditions the device is intended for, not only under laboratory conditions. Not yet started
Skin safety Rates of irritation, contact dermatitis, and adverse skin events, including in infants. In progress
What CF-ARIA does not yet demonstrate

No peer-reviewed diagnostic accuracy data for CF-ARIA has been published. Any description of the device's performance on this site should be read as a design objective, not as a demonstrated result.

Where results will appear

Publications, conference presentations, and trial registrations will be listed here as they become available, so that the evidence base can be inspected directly rather than summarised.

Common Questions

These are the objections we expect, and the honest answers to them at the current stage of development.

Sweat chloride testing already works. Why does this need to exist?

It does work, and CF-ARIA is not proposed as a replacement for it where it is available. The gap is geographic and economic rather than technical. Quantitative pilocarpine iontophoresis needs equipment, consumables, and trained staff, and is concentrated in a small number of tertiary centres. In one survey of Indian paediatricians, 90 percent reported having no access to sweat chloride testing or genetic studies, and 87.5 percent said that unavailability made diagnosing CF difficult.3

If a patient can reach a centre that performs sweat testing regularly, that test remains the right one.

Where would this sit in a diagnostic pathway?

The intended position is triage rather than confirmation: a test that can be performed outside a specialist laboratory to identify who warrants referral for formal sweat testing and genetic analysis. On that model a positive result initiates a diagnostic pathway; it does not conclude one.

This is a lower evidentiary bar than replacing the reference standard, and it is a more realistic claim. Whether the data supports even that is what validation has to determine.

What happens if the result is wrong?

The two errors carry different costs. A false negative returns a patient to the diagnostic delay the device exists to shorten, which is the more serious failure given that undiagnosed CF in this population frequently presents later with advanced malnutrition, pseudo-Bartter syndrome, or established Pseudomonas colonisation.4

A false positive generates referral, anxiety, and a confirmatory test that will correct it. In a triage model that is the tolerable error, which is why threshold selection should favour sensitivity over specificity. Quantifying both is part of validation.

How much training and time does it require?

Application time, wear duration, and the training required to obtain a reliable result are part of the technical specification and will be stated once the laboratory has finalised the protocol. Workflow burden is a legitimate reason to reject a diagnostic, and it will be documented rather than glossed over.

Is this a CFTR modulator eligibility test, or a diagnostic?

A diagnostic. Sweat chloride does fall measurably on modulator therapy, which makes serial measurement an interesting secondary possibility, but that is a research question rather than a current claim. Modulator eligibility is determined by genotype, and no sweat-based measurement substitutes for sequencing.2

Can I use it in my clinic now?

No. CF-ARIA has not been approved or cleared by any regulatory authority and is not available for clinical use. The routes currently open to clinicians are research collaboration and providing design input, both described below.
